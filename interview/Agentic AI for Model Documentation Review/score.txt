Below is a practical **assessment framework** you can use across both:

* the **main case study questions**
* the **12 High-Value Practical Challenge Questions**

I’ve structured it so you can score consistently during interview and quickly distinguish:

* genuine hands-on candidates
* strong but more conceptual candidates
* weak / buzzword-driven candidates

---

# 1. Overall scoring rubric

Use a **1–5 scale** for each question or dimension.

## Score 5 — Strong

* Clear, structured, practical answer
* Shows real implementation judgement
* Understands trade-offs and failure modes
* Distinguishes automation vs human judgement well
* Thinks in workflows, states, objects, traceability, and controls
* Gives concrete examples of system behavior

## Score 4 — Good

* Strong conceptual answer with solid practical thinking
* Covers most important points
* Some implementation depth, though not always highly specific
* Minor gaps, but overall credible and usable

## Score 3 — Acceptable

* Reasonable answer, but more high-level than practical
* Understands core ideas, but limited system-design depth
* Some important points missing
* Could likely contribute, but may need support in delivery

## Score 2 — Weak

* Partial understanding only
* Generic or vague response
* Misses important control, workflow, or practical considerations
* Relies too much on buzzwords or surface-level RAG/agent language

## Score 1 — Poor

* Does not understand the problem well
* Treats the case like a generic chatbot
* No clear separation of rules, retrieval, LLMs, human review
* No practical design, controls, or delivery thinking

# 2. Main case study questions: scoring rubric + strong / weak signals

---

## Q1. Product framing

**How would you define this product, and what problem is it solving for a model validation team?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Frames it clearly as a **review support platform** for standards-driven documentation review, focused on consistency, evidence gaps, follow-up, repeat review, and validator support without replacing judgement.
**3:** Understands it as a documentation review tool, but more like document Q&A or summarisation support.
**1:** Frames it as a generic chatbot over uploaded documents.

### Strong answer signals

* Says this is **not a general chatbot**
* Focuses on **review workflow**
* Mentions **evidence sufficiency**, **documentation gaps**, **follow-up**
* Mentions **repeat review / resubmission**
* Says platform should improve **consistency and efficiency**, not replace validators

### Weak answer signals

* Says “it answers questions over documents”
* Focuses only on search, summarisation, or chatbot UX
* No mention of standards / expectations / review workflow
* No mention of evidence gaps or repeated review
* Implies LLM can make final validation judgement

---

## Q2. User journeys

**What are the main user journeys in this platform?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Describes a full lifecycle: upload, setup, review run, evidence drill-down, comment/override, resubmission, rerun, compare versions, close/archive.
**3:** Covers upload and review, but misses rerun, version comparison, or close-out flows.
**1:** Only describes asking questions about documents.

### Strong answer signals

* Upload / intake
* Review run
* Review findings
* Evidence drill-down
* Comment / override
* Resubmission / rerun
* Compare versions
* Close / archive

### Weak answer signals

* Only talks about one-step “upload and get answer”
* No reviewer workflow
* No override / comment flow
* No iterative review cycle
* No archive / history

---

## Q3. End-to-end architecture

**How would you design the end-to-end architecture?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Breaks architecture into clear layers: ingestion, parsing, normalisation, chunking/section mapping, review orchestration, rules, optional retrieval, semantic checks, findings store, UI/API, persistence, audit/logging.
**3:** Covers some major components, but lacks modular separation or persistence/audit depth.
**1:** Mentions only vector DB + LLM + frontend.

### Strong answer signals

* Clear layered architecture
* Separates parsing, orchestration, persistence
* Talks about core objects and services
* Includes findings store and audit trail
* Includes UI / API / export / reporting

### Weak answer signals

* Only says “store embeddings and ask model”
* No persistence design
* No orchestration logic
* No findings object model
* No separation of concerns

---

## Q4. Rules, retrieval, LLMs, and human review

**Where should deterministic rules, retrieval, LLM semantic reasoning, and human review each fit?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Clearly separates each role and gives realistic examples of what belongs where; keeps final judgement with humans.
**3:** Understands some separation, but overlaps components or leaves too much to the LLM.
**1:** Treats the LLM as the primary engine for everything.

### Strong answer signals

* Rules for completeness / structure / metadata
* Retrieval for standards / expectations / guidance
* LLM for clarity / consistency / sufficiency / mapping
* Human for sign-off / override / ambiguity / materiality
* States clearly what should **never** be left solely to the model

### Weak answer signals

* “Let the model check everything”
* No clear role for rules
* No retrieval role beyond generic context stuffing
* Lets agent produce final validation conclusion
* No human-in-the-loop boundary

---

## Q5. Why agentic AI here?

**Why is this an agentic AI use case rather than a simple pipeline or one-shot prompt?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Explains where adaptive routing, stateful reruns, multi-step review, requirement-based tool use, and human loop justify agentic behavior, while avoiding over-engineering.
**3:** Understands there are multiple steps, but gives fuzzy reasons for “agentic.”
**1:** Says it is agentic just because it uses an LLM and tools.

### Strong answer signals

* Multiple steps
* Branching logic
* Adaptive tool use
* Requirement-based routing
* Human input and reruns
* Stateful workflow
* Also knows when deterministic pipeline is enough

### Weak answer signals

* Agentic = “fancier AI”
* No explanation of what is adaptive
* No workflow/state reasoning
* Wants agent for everything
* No awareness of complexity cost

---

## Q6. Parsing and evidence traceability

**How would you design parsing and evidence traceability for long model documents?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Discusses parser/OCR choice by document type, structure preservation, tables/appendices, anchors/page references, extraction quality checks, and evidence linkage.
**3:** Understands parsing matters, but gives only generic OCR / text extraction comments.
**1:** Assumes documents can simply be embedded as raw text.

### Strong answer signals

* Parser choice depends on file type and quality
* OCR only when needed
* Preserve headings, sections, tables, appendices
* Preserve page / anchor / source references
* Extraction quality gating
* Traceability from findings back to source

### Weak answer signals

* “Just OCR it”
* No structure preservation
* No table/appendix handling
* No extraction quality checks
* No grounding back to original doc

---

## Q7. Chunking and review unit design

**How would you split documents into review units without losing validation context?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Proposes section-aware, hierarchy-aware chunking with metadata and different treatment for narrative vs tabular evidence; addresses cross-section consistency risks.
**3:** Understands chunking matters, but falls back on token-size-only logic.
**1:** Uses arbitrary fixed-size chunking with no regard for document structure.

### Strong answer signals

* Section-aware chunking
* Hierarchy-aware chunking
* Document-schema-aware if possible
* Keeps evidence linkage and section metadata
* Avoids splitting logical units badly
* Recognises some checks must span sections/documents

### Weak answer signals

* “Chunk every 500 tokens”
* No section context
* No handling for narrative vs tables
* No cross-section consistency thinking
* No debugging approach for chunking failures

---

## Q8. Structured findings

**What should a structured review finding look like?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Describes a concrete schema with IDs, run/document references, category, severity, rule/review-pack refs, evidence links, rationale, recommendations, status, comments, timestamps.
**3:** Understands findings should be structured, but schema is incomplete.
**1:** Only describes narrative text output.

### Strong answer signals

* finding_id / run_id
* category / severity
* rule or review-pack reference
* description / rationale
* evidence reference(s)
* recommendation / next action
* status
* reviewer decision / comment
* timestamps

### Weak answer signals

* Only suggests free-text summary
* No evidence fields
* No reviewer action fields
* No status / lifecycle
* No distinction between deterministic and inferred concerns

---

## Q9. Repeat review cycles and persistence

**How would you support repeated review after the model owner updates documents?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Explains versioning, run lineage, delta comparison, resolved/reopened findings, preservation of comments/overrides, and targeted vs full rerun logic.
**3:** Recognises reruns matter, but persistence and lineage design are weak.
**1:** Treats review as a one-shot process.

### Strong answer signals

* Document versioning
* Review run lineage
* Delta comparison
* Resolved / reopened / unchanged findings
* Preserve comments and overrides
* Partial rerun where appropriate
* Clear case-level vs run-level data

### Weak answer signals

* No history model
* Rerun just overwrites old results
* Overrides disappear
* No version comparison
* No notion of reopened findings

---

## Q10. Controls and evaluation

**What are the main risks in this platform, and how would you evaluate quality?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Identifies parser/rule/model/control risks and proposes evaluation across benchmark cases, expected findings, evidence correctness, acceptance/override rates, rerun consistency, and SME review.
**3:** Mentions some risks and vague evaluation, but lacks practical measurement design.
**1:** Says “test accuracy” without specifics.

### Strong answer signals

* Parser errors
* Wrong rules
* Hallucinated findings
* Weak evidence linkage
* False sense of completeness
* Stale standards
* Access control
* Inconsistent reruns
* Benchmark datasets
* Expected findings
* Evidence correctness
* Acceptance / override rates
* Rerun consistency

### Weak answer signals

* Only mentions hallucination
* No parser/rules/governance risk
* No benchmark idea
* No reviewer/SME feedback loop
* No practical metrics

---

## Q11. Delivery leadership

**If you were leading this workstream, what would you deliver in the first 8–12 weeks?**

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Proposes a tightly scoped MVP with one or two document types, one review pack, basic parsing, structured findings, evidence drill-down, comments/overrides, persistent history, exportable report, and sensible sequencing.
**3:** Has some roadmap thinking, but scope is too broad or too vague.
**1:** Either no MVP thinking or proposes a full end-state platform immediately.

### Strong answer signals

* Narrow MVP
* One or two document types first
* One review pack first
* Basic parsing + findings + evidence drill-down
* Comments and override
* Persistent history
* Exportable report
* Pilot then controlled rollout

### Weak answer signals

* Tries to do everything at once
* No prioritisation
* No team/dependency awareness
* No pilot strategy
* No deliberate exclusions from MVP

# 3. High-Value Practical Challenge Questions: scoring rubric + strong / weak signals

---

## Practical 1. Rules vs semantic interpretation conflict

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Keeps deterministic and semantic signals separate, marks ambiguity explicitly, shows both to reviewer, and does not silently override the rule.
**3:** Recognises conflict but gives a generic “ask user/reviewer to decide.”
**1:** Lets the LLM override the deterministic result automatically.

### Strong answer signals

* Separate finding types/bases
* Mapping ambiguity classification
* Evidence shown side by side
* Reviewer confirmation required
* Improvement loop for mapping logic later

### Weak answer signals

* “Trust the LLM if it seems reasonable”
* One result overwrites the other
* No explicit ambiguity state
* No reviewer-facing behavior described

---

## Practical 2. Parser failure on critical appendix/table

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Uses extraction quality gating, blocks/downgrades dependent checks, allows unaffected checks to continue, requests manual intervention, and avoids fabricated certainty.
**3:** Recognises parser issue but only says “flag an error.”
**1:** Continues review as normal and generates confident findings.

### Strong answer signals

* Extraction quality flag
* Dependent checks blocked or downgraded
* Unaffected checks may continue
* Manual review / better upload requested
* Parser failure logged clearly

### Weak answer signals

* Ignores extraction confidence
* Treats all checks equally
* Still produces strong findings from corrupted table
* No degradation/fallback logic

---

## Practical 3. Binding findings to source location

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Preserves parse-time anchors such as page, section, chunk ID, bbox/table cell refs; stores these in finding schema and supports source drill-down.
**3:** Mentions citations or page numbers only.
**1:** No real source-grounding approach.

### Strong answer signals

* Page / section / anchor / chunk ID
* Bbox or cell refs where possible
* Parse-time artifact preservation
* Finding schema includes evidence refs
* Supports source highlighting

### Weak answer signals

* “Just add citation text”
* No parse-time planning
* No precision handling
* No source navigation behavior

---

## Practical 4. Right standard retrieved, poor final comment

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Debugs in layers with traces/logs: retrieval, reranking, requirement mapping, chunking, prompt assembly, truncation, citation mismatch, schema/post-processing.
**3:** Says “inspect prompt and retrieval” but not much deeper.
**1:** Immediately changes model or prompt without diagnosis.

### Strong answer signals

* Layered debug strategy
* Trace/run ID usage
* Requirement mapping inspection
* Evidence-summary mismatch analysis
* Doesn’t jump straight to model swap

### Weak answer signals

* “Tune the prompt”
* “Use a better model”
* No traceability
* No structured failure analysis

---

## Practical 5. OCR/text+retrieval vs end-to-end VLM

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Explains trade-offs around token cost, document length, chunking control, traceability, auditability, section handling, and balanced VLM use cases.
**3:** Gives one-sided answer with some correct points.
**1:** Treats newer multimodal models as automatically better.

### Strong answer signals

* Token/cost awareness
* Better evidence traceability
* Better section/chunk control
* Easier auditability
* Balanced judgement on when VLM still helps

### Weak answer signals

* “Use VLM for everything”
* No traceability argument
* No long-document or cost thinking
* No document-control perspective

---

## Practical 6. Reviewer override reappears on rerun

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Preserves override as an explicit historical decision, separates new system result from old reviewer decision, and uses reconciliation states like reopened/previously overridden.
**3:** Understands overrides should not disappear, but lacks concrete rerun logic.
**1:** New rerun simply replaces prior human decision.

### Strong answer signals

* Override stored explicitly
* New and prior states both visible
* Reconciliation logic
* Historical lineage preserved
* Different treatment for changed vs unchanged content

### Weak answer signals

* Overrides lost
* Auto-reapply without conditions
* No lineage
* No reopened state

---

## Practical 7. Full rerun vs impacted rerun

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Uses diffing and dependency logic, reruns impacted checks first, reruns global consistency checks when needed, and falls back to full rerun when confidence is weak.
**3:** Understands the trade-off but no concrete dependency logic.
**1:** Always reruns everything or always reruns only changed sections without nuance.

### Strong answer signals

* Section-level diffing
* Check dependency awareness
* Global vs local rerun distinction
* Safe fallback to full rerun
* Balances efficiency and correctness

### Weak answer signals

* No dependency thinking
* Overly simplistic rerun policy
* No fallback conditions
* No confidence/uncertainty handling

---

## Practical 8. Cross-document inconsistency

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Creates explicit cross-document consistency finding, cites both sources, avoids false certainty, and escalates to reviewer/follow-up when needed.
**3:** Notices inconsistency, but response is vague.
**1:** Chooses one document arbitrarily or ignores inconsistency.

### Strong answer signals

* Structured cross-document finding
* Both sources referenced
* No forced auto-resolution
* Reviewer follow-up / escalation
* Optional targeted consistency checks

### Weak answer signals

* Picks one answer without basis
* No structured finding
* No reviewer review step
* No evidence comparison

---

## Practical 9. Too many low-value findings

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Uses override/dismissal/acceptance metrics, parser and chunking review, rule/prompt tuning, scope tightening, and distinction between observations and findings.
**3:** Recognises precision/noise issue, but improvement plan is vague.
**1:** Only suggests using a better model or fine-tuning.

### Strong answer signals

* Noise diagnosis by finding type
* Reviewer behavior metrics
* Parser/chunking inspection
* Precision-focused refinement
* Separates informational observations from findings

### Weak answer signals

* “Fine-tune the model”
* No metrics
* No analysis by category
* No threshold/scope control

---

## Practical 10. Logging for debug, audit, and improvement

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Proposes comprehensive observability including IDs, versions, parser outputs/confidence, rule versions, retrieval hits, prompts, outputs, findings lifecycle, overrides, lineage, and distinguishes audit vs debug logs.
**3:** Mentions logs generally, but not what should be logged.
**1:** No clear observability plan.

### Strong answer signals

* Case / submission / version / run IDs
* Parser artifacts/confidence
* Rule/review-pack versioning
* Retrieval and prompt traces
* Findings lifecycle
* Comments / overrides
* Rerun lineage
* Audit vs debug distinction

### Weak answer signals

* “Log errors”
* No entity/version awareness
* No prompt/retrieval traces
* No audit trail concept

---

## Practical 11. Confidence without false reassurance

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Avoids naive confidence score, prefers interpretable reliability signals like extraction quality, evidence coverage, inferred vs deterministic basis, ambiguity/conflict flags, and “needs reviewer confirmation.”
**3:** Understands confidence is tricky, but answer remains generic.
**1:** Suggests a single model confidence score as the main control.

### Strong answer signals

* Interpretable reliability signals
* “Insufficient evidence” state
* Extraction quality and evidence coverage
* Ambiguity/conflict indicators
* Reviewer confirmation flags

### Weak answer signals

* One opaque confidence number
* False precision
* No reliability decomposition
* No ambiguity state

---

## Practical 12. 6-week MVP: hard-coded vs configurable

### Scoring rubric

**5:** Shows pragmatic sequencing: hard-codes narrow scope for speed, keeps selected components configurable for future growth, and avoids premature platform overbuild.
**3:** Has some MVP thinking, but config vs hard-code split is weak.
**1:** Either over-engineers a configurable platform immediately or hard-codes everything with no evolution path.

### Strong answer signals

* One review pack
* One or two document types
* Limited workflow states/checks
* Simple finding schema
* Prompts / rule wording / pack definitions configurable where sensible
* Clear delivery pragmatism

### Weak answer signals

* Build full platform first
* No MVP boundary
* No configurability thinking
* No practical trade-off awareness

# 4. Recommended score dimensions for the whole interview

If you want a simpler way to score across all questions, use these **6 dimensions** and score each 1–5.

## 1. Product framing and workflow understanding

### Strong signals

* Understands this is a review platform, not chatbot
* Clear lifecycle and user journey thinking

### Weak signals

* Generic document Q&A framing

## 2. Practical system design depth

### Strong signals

* Concrete architecture, object model, persistence, findings schema

### Weak signals

* High-level component listing only

## 3. Agentic workflow judgement

### Strong signals

* Knows when adaptive orchestration helps and when it does not

### Weak signals

* Agentic buzzwords with no control or state logic

## 4. Evidence traceability and controls

### Strong signals

* Source grounding, extraction quality, auditability, HITL boundaries

### Weak signals

* No traceability or governance thinking

## 5. Debugging and operational thinking

### Strong signals

* Layered failure analysis, logs, traces, rerun logic, fallback behavior

### Weak signals

* Only says “change model/prompt”

## 6. Delivery leadership and pragmatism

### Strong signals

* Narrow MVP, prioritisation, phased rollout, dependency awareness

### Weak signals

* Big-bang design, no sequencing

# 5. Overall strong candidate profile

A strong candidate usually:

* frames the product as a **standards-driven review platform**
* separates **rules, retrieval, semantic reasoning, and human decisions**
* understands **parsing, chunking, evidence mapping, reruns, and persistence**
* answers practical questions in terms of:

  * system behavior
  * reviewer experience
  * what gets stored
  * how the platform degrades safely
* shows **debugging discipline**
* shows **MVP judgement**
* avoids overclaiming what AI should automate

# 6. Overall weak candidate profile

A weak candidate usually:

* treats everything as generic RAG/chatbot
* overuses buzzwords like agent, vector DB, prompt engineering
* does not discuss:

  * reviewer override
  * reruns
  * evidence traceability
  * structured findings
  * audit trail
* lets the LLM make final decisions
* cannot explain failure handling or operational debugging
* has no prioritised MVP or rollout thinking
